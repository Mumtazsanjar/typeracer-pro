What Is a Good Typing Speed? Average WPM Explained

The question "what's a good WPM?" doesn't have a single answer — it depends on your context, your goals, and what you're comparing against. This article explains what the numbers actually mean, where different speeds fit in the real world, and what a realistic target looks like for you.

How Typing Speed Is Measured

Typing speed is measured in Words Per Minute (WPM). Despite the name, WPM doesn't count actual words — it uses a normalised definition where every 5 characters, including spaces and punctuation, equals one word. This keeps scores consistent regardless of whether you typed short simple words or long technical ones.

The formula is: (correct characters ÷ 5) ÷ minutes elapsed. Only correctly typed characters count. Errors are tracked separately as your accuracy percentage — so WPM and accuracy together give a complete picture of your typing performance.

You can read more about the formula in the WPM test guide or try the WPM calculator to convert any character count and time into a WPM figure.

Typing Speed Ranges at a Glance

These ranges reflect general real-world usage. Precise "global average" figures aren't reliably measured across all populations, so treat these as practical reference points rather than hard statistical thresholds.

WPM Range Level What it means in practice
Under 30 WPM Beginner Slower than the general adult average; typing is a conscious effort on every word
30–50 WPM Average Around the commonly cited adult average; functional for most everyday tasks
50–70 WPM Good Above average; comfortable for professional work including emails, documents and code
70–100 WPM Fast Significantly faster than most; typical of regular touch typists and heavy keyboard users
100–120 WPM Professional Very fast; the result of deliberate long-term practice and solid touch typing technique
120+ WPM Expert Exceptional speed; competitive typists, stenographers, and highly practised individuals

Note: These ranges are reference points, not official statistics. Individual speeds vary widely by age, keyboard type, native language, and how much time a person spends typing daily.

Beginner Typing Speed (Under 30 WPM)

At under 30 WPM, typing is still an active cognitive task — you're consciously thinking about where each key is rather than letting fingers find them automatically. This is normal for anyone who hasn't developed a consistent typing habit yet.

The most common reasons for staying in this range are hunt-and-peck typing (using two to four fingers and looking at the keyboard), infrequent computer use, or having learned to type informally without any structured technique.

The good news: beginners see the fastest gains. Moving from 20 WPM to 40 WPM typically takes a few weeks of focused daily practice — far less time than moving from 60 to 80 WPM later on. Starting with structured typing practice at Easy difficulty and prioritising accuracy over speed produces rapid, visible improvement.

Average Typing Speed (30–50 WPM)

The commonly referenced average typing speed for adults is around 40 WPM. This figure appears across typing tools, educational resources, and office productivity studies, though it isn't derived from a single large-scale global study. Treat it as a reasonable ballpark rather than a precise scientific threshold.

At 40–50 WPM, typing is functional but not frictionless. You can write emails, fill in forms, and produce documents at a reasonable pace. It's enough for most casual computing, but if your job involves hours of writing each day, the time cost of a lower speed adds up noticeably over a week.

If you're currently in this range and want to improve, the biggest lever is technique rather than practice volume. Switching to touch typing — using all ten fingers from home-row positions — typically doubles speed within a few months for typists in this range.

Fast Typing Speed (50–100 WPM)

Typing between 50 and 100 WPM puts you comfortably above the general adult average. In this range, typing starts to feel more automatic — you're no longer consciously thinking about key locations for most common words.

50–70 WPM: Good

This is a solid professional range. Documents, emails, and code flow at a comfortable pace. Errors are manageable and don't significantly interrupt workflow. Most people who've made a deliberate effort to learn proper technique end up here.

70–100 WPM: Fast

At 70–100 WPM, you're typing faster than the vast majority of general computer users. Reaching this range requires touch typing to be second nature — hands on home row, eyes on screen, minimal hesitation. Writers, developers, and anyone who spends most of their working day at a keyboard often settle in this range after years of regular use.

Professional and Expert Speeds (100+ WPM)

Reaching 100 WPM and above is the result of deliberate, sustained practice — not just frequent typing. Many people use keyboards for years without getting past 60–70 WPM because they never addressed their technique. Getting to 100+ WPM requires intentional work: consistent daily practice, focus on accuracy, and progressive difficulty.

Above 120 WPM, the landscape becomes specialist. Competitive typists train specifically to push WPM records. Stenographers and court reporters use chorded keyboards and can record speech in real time at 200+ WPM — a fundamentally different skill from standard QWERTY typing.

For most people, a realistic and highly useful goal is 70–90 WPM at 97%+ accuracy. That's fast enough to eliminate typing as a bottleneck in almost any professional context, and it's achievable for most adults within 6–12 months of consistent practice.

Why Accuracy Matters as Much as Speed

A WPM score only tells half the story. Two people can both score 60 WPM but produce very different results depending on their accuracy. At 95% accuracy, 60 WPM produces about 57 correct words per minute. At 80% accuracy, it produces around 48 — and the time spent correcting errors means actual output is lower still.

In real work, accuracy matters beyond just time:

  • A typo in code can introduce a bug that takes an hour to track down
  • Errors in professional communications affect credibility
  • In data entry, accuracy errors often have direct consequences

The practical standard most typing resources and professionals use is 95% accuracy or above at any WPM level. Below that, the speed gains are partially cancelled by correction overhead. If your accuracy is below 90%, it's worth slowing down until it stabilises before pushing for more speed.

How to Improve Your Typing Speed

Regardless of where you currently sit on the range above, the same principles apply:

  • Learn touch typing — assigning all ten fingers to their home-row positions and typing without looking at the keyboard is the highest-impact change most people can make
  • Accuracy before speed — aim for 95%+ accuracy at a comfortable pace before pushing faster; speed follows naturally from clean, accurate typing
  • Practise daily — 10–15 minutes every day produces far better results than occasional long sessions; muscle memory is built through repetition over time
  • Progress through difficulty — start with short common words, then move to full sentences, then harder technical vocabulary as your accuracy stabilises
  • Track your results — use a consistent test duration (60s or 120s) and note your WPM and accuracy weekly to see the trend
